Architecture

Ten judges run as native AgentCore code-based evaluators. AgentCore owns selection, sampling and write-back; a Lambda owns the judging, which is what lets the existing Anthropic pipeline survive intact.

Why the Lambda, not the native LLM judge

the decision everything follows from

AgentCore's native llmAsAJudge — rejected

It appends its own standardisation prompt forcing {reasoning, score}, tells you not to write output-format instructions, and restricts prompts to a closed placeholder set — {context}, {assistant_turn}, {tool_turn}. There is no way to pass a pre-rendered transcript, and the rubrics name their markers explicitly. Its inferenceConfig also exposes only maxTokens/temperature/topP: no thinking control, no cache control, no output schema.

codeBased Lambda — chosen

You construct the request, so every capability survives: json_schema output validated at generation time, effort: high, prompt caching on the shared preamble, and k=5 with a median and spread. The collapse to three fields happens on return, after validation.

The asymmetry that decides it. Arize's equivalent — custom code evaluators — was rejected because Arize has no secret store: an ANTHROPIC_API_KEY would sit in plaintext, permanently versioned in the Eval Hub. A Lambda has an execution role and reads the key from Secrets Manager. That single difference retires the fatal limit from the Arize integration, where Anthropic effort: high is simply unreachable.

Sizing risk to measure first. k=5 concurrent Opus calls at effort: high on a ~19k-token prompt must finish inside the Lambda's 300-second ceiling. Concurrent, that is roughly one call's latency plus jitter — plausible, not comfortable. Fallbacks in order: drop to k=3 in production and keep k=5/k=10 offline on the anchor set; fetch the transcript from your own store instead of parsing 6 MB of spans; or split the two hardest judges into their own evaluators with separate budgets.
